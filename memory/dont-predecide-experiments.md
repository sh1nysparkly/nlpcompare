Anna builds experimental apparatus to test hypotheses; her instinct is to run experiments and look, not to act on tentative findings. When she shares a partial result or a hypothesis-worth-testing, treat it as a hypothesis worth testing, not a recommendation to make. Don't write conclusions ("recipe is safe", "cut popular-destinations", "nuke the sibling promo block") into status docs, summaries, or per-page writeups until she has signed off explicitly. Status doc claims become things she has to walk back to UX/colleagues -- a real cost.

**Why:** May 8, 2026 session. I labeled the UX recipe NLP test "Green light for UX. Recipe is safe." in `signal-coherence/SIGNAL-COHERENCE-STATUS.md` without her sign-off, then in the same conversation moved straight from her ablation results to "Tier 1 sneakable: nuke the sibling promo block on car-rental-insurance" -- twice in one session, on hypotheses she explicitly wanted to test, not act on. She caught both. Her actual stance: "we DEFFO don't have conclusive enough data to say: yep let's can the destinations from Cruise entirely. Like I THINK so. But. Let's. Test." The whole Signal Coherence Framework was built so she could experiment -- the apparatus exists so she can run tests, not so I can pre-decide outcomes. Her phrasing: "My dream as a little girl was to grow up to be a mad scientist so let me frickin' experiment!"

**How to apply:** When working on any test/comparison/ablation: present results as evidence about a question, not as a decision. Use "the data suggests / one hypothesis is / this is consistent with" rather than "do X / cut Y / safe to ship." Reserve recommendations for explicit "what should I do" requests, and even then, frame as hypotheses to test next, not actions to take. Never write conclusions into status docs or findings docs unless she has signed off explicitly. When a result feels like it points clearly somewhere, the move is "want to test this further?" not "Tier 1 recommendation." Specifically with NLP work: it's a clarity diagnostic ("the page becomes measurably more focused on X"), not a ranking predictor ("this will rank better"). The first is defensible. The second exposes her.
